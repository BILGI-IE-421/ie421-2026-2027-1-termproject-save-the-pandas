Restless Earth: Understanding the Impacts of Significant Earthquakes

Earthquakes are natural events, but their consequences are shaped by the places and communities they affect. This project explores how reported human and economic losses vary across income groups and how reported deaths relate to earthquake magnitude and recorded tsunami association.

Team: Save the Pandas

What does the data tell us?

We combine significant earthquake records from NOAA with country-year GDP per capita data from the World Bank and Consumer Price Index data from FRED. The visualizations are descriptive: they show patterns in the available records and do not establish that income level or tsunami association causes a particular level of loss.

Research question 1

How do human and economic impacts differ across GDP per capita groups?

Earthquake records are grouped into four quartiles according to the corresponding country-year GDP per capita. We compare the median reported deaths and median reported economic damage in each group.

How to read the figure

The left panel shows that median reported deaths are higher in the lowest GDP quartile (Q1) than in the highest quartile (Q4): 9 versus 3 deaths per recorded event. The right panel shows the opposite pattern for reported economic damage: the median rises from about 20.7 million 2024 USD in Q1 to about 177.5 million 2024 USD in Q4. These are differences in the recorded event-level medians, not estimates of each country's overall earthquake risk.

Limitations to keep in mind

  • GDP quartiles are calculated across earthquake records with available GDP data. They are country-year groups, not permanent labels for countries.
  • Only positive reported death and damage values are plotted. Missing or unreported values are not treated as zero; events without an outcome value are excluded from that panel.
  • Damage is adjusted using the US CPI series. This provides a common 2024-dollar reference but does not account for country-specific inflation or differences in asset prices.
  • Economic losses depend on the value and exposure of assets, reporting coverage, and other factors—not only on GDP per capita.
  • Events from the same country may not be independent. The intervals resample earthquake records, not whole countries.
Research question 2

How are earthquake magnitude and reported human losses related across tsunami groups?

This figure compares average reported deaths across magnitude intervals for earthquakes with and without a recorded NOAA tsunami association. It focuses on earthquakes with magnitudes from 7.0 to 9.0.

How to read the figure

The two lines fluctuate substantially rather than following a steady upward or downward pattern. In some magnitude intervals, the mean reported deaths are higher among tsunami-associated events; in others, the non-tsunami-associated group has the higher mean. This suggests that magnitude alone does not describe the variation in reported human losses in this sample. The number of events differs between intervals, so individual points should be interpreted alongside their sample sizes.

Limitations to keep in mind

  • The analysis includes only events with a recorded positive death count. Events with missing death information and zero values are excluded; therefore, the figure does not represent all significant earthquakes.
  • The vertical axis starts at 5 rather than zero. This truncated scale makes small differences near the bottom easier to see, but can visually exaggerate differences.
  • The mean is sensitive to a small number of earthquakes with very high death tolls. Some magnitude intervals contain very few observations, especially at the upper end.
  • The magnitude intervals are 0.25 units wide in this figure. They are not half-unit bands.
  • This figure covers human losses only. It does not show the economic-loss comparison, and it does not by itself complete a research question that asks about both human and economic losses.
  • A recorded tsunami association indicates a link in the NOAA data; it does not prove that a tsunami caused the reported deaths.
Interpretation note: The current chart is an exploratory comparison of mean deaths by magnitude interval. If the final RQ explicitly requires comparing both human and economic losses within the same half-unit magnitude band, the analysis and figure should be extended to include economic damage and use half-unit bands before making that claim.
Methodology

Data sources and overall limitations

The analysis uses significant earthquake records from 1960–2024. Country names are matched to World Bank ISO3 codes to merge annual GDP per capita. Monthly FRED CPI observations are averaged by year and used to express reported damage in 2024 US dollars. Records without a magnitude are removed during preprocessing. Missing values are retained as missing rather than being converted to zero.

NOAA Significant Earthquake Database — event characteristics, tsunami association, reported deaths and damage.
World Bank GDP per capita (constant 2015 US$), indicator NY.GDP.PCAP.KD.
FRED CPI for All Urban Consumers: All Items in U.S. City Average (CPIAUCNS), used for the damage adjustment.

This is an observational dataset of significant earthquakes, not a complete catalogue of every earthquake. Reporting practices and data availability may differ across countries and years. The figures describe associations in the available records; they do not establish causal relationships.
